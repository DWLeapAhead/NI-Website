IT Act 2000 & IT Amendment Act 2008: Penalties, Offences With Case Studies

How India's foundational technology law defines cybercrime, assigns penalties, and has been applied in practice.

Lionel Faleiro · Updated 2026

Why India needed the IT Act

The Information Technology Act 2000 gave legal recognition to electronic transactions — e-commerce conducted through electronic data interchange rather than paper — and allowed electronic filing with government agencies. It also amended the Indian Penal Code, the Indian Evidence Act, the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, and the RBI Act to accommodate digital records.

The Act covers legal recognition of electronic documents and digital signatures, defines offences and contraventions, and sets up justice systems for cybercrime.

Why the 2008 Amendment arose

The original 2000 Act was India's first technology and e-commerce law, and it showed its age quickly: investigators leaned on a century-old Indian Penal Code for tech-related cases, and critics called parts of it both too harsh and too weak. Industry consultation led to a comprehensive amendment that passed in late 2008, received presidential assent in February 2009, and took effect in October 2009.

Notable additions included a data privacy and information security focus, a legal definition of cyber cafés, technology-neutral digital signatures, defined "reasonable security practices" for corporations, redefined intermediary roles, formal recognition of CERT-In, new offences including child pornography and cyber terrorism, and authority for Inspectors — not just DSPs — to investigate cyber offences.

How the Act is structured

Thirteen chapters and 90 sections, with Sections 91–94 amending four other laws. It covers electronic records and signatures, certifying authorities, civil data theft, adjudication and appeals, specific cybercrimes and their punishments, due diligence, intermediary obligations, and miscellaneous provisions. It applies across India and, with exceptions, to offences committed abroad by any person, though certain instruments — non-cheque negotiable instruments, powers of attorney, trusts, wills, and certain property contracts — are excluded.

What counts as cybercrime

No single official definition exists in the IT Act or elsewhere. In the narrow sense, it's illegal acts targeting computer systems and the data they process. In the broad sense, it's illegal acts committed using or relating to computer systems or networks, including distributing illegal information.

Case studies by section

  • Section 43 (damage to computers): the Mphasis BPO fraud (2005), where call-centre staff stole customer PINs and moved funds.
  • Section 65 (source document tampering): Syed Asifuddin v. State of Andhra Pradesh, involving manipulated cell phone identification numbers.
  • Section 66 (computer offences): Kumar v. Whiteley, a hacker who accessed a broadband network and altered accounts.
  • Section 66A (offensive messages): a fake presidential profile on Facebook (2010) and a 2009 bomb hoax email sent by a teenager.
  • Section 66C (identity theft): a LifeLock CEO's exposed SSN used for a loan, and a graduate student who faked his death to obtain documents.
  • Section 66D (cheating by impersonation): Sandeep Varghese v. State of Kerala, a fake website and emails used to defame a company.
  • Section 66E (privacy violation): the JNU MMS scandal and a Nagpur MMS case.
  • Section 66F (cyber terrorism): a threat email to BSE and NSE, traced to Patna.
  • Section 67 (obscene material): a harassment case involving a fake Yahoo group — India's first conviction under this section.
  • Section 69 (interception and decryption): the wrongful arrest of Lakshmana Kailash K. over Orkut content, caused by an erroneous ISP IP record, who received ₹2 lakh in damages.

Common scenarios and applicable sections

  • Fake social media profiles: Sections 66A, 67, and IPC 509
  • Email account hacking: Sections 43, 66, 66A, 66C, 67, 67A, 67B
  • Credit card fraud: Sections 43, 66, 66C, 66D, and IPC 420
  • Malware: Sections 43, 66, 66A, and IPC 426
  • Phishing and email scams: Sections 66, 66A, 66D, and IPC 420
  • Theft of confidential information: Sections 43, 66, 66B, and IPC 426

Key penalty sections at a glance

Section 43 covers compensation for computer damage; 43A covers compensation for failing to protect sensitive data, capped at ₹5 crore; 44 and 45 cover penalties for failing to furnish information and residuary penalties. Sections 65 through 67B cover the core offences — tampering, computer offences, offensive messages, identity theft, impersonation, privacy violations, cyber terrorism, and obscene or child-related material. Sections 69–69B cover interception, blocking, and traffic monitoring; 75 gives the Act extraterritorial reach; and 78 authorizes investigation by an Inspector-rank officer or higher.

Frequently asked

What is the difference between the IT Act 2000 and the 2008 Amendment?

The 2000 Act gave legal recognition to electronic transactions and signatures. The 2008 Amendment added data privacy obligations, new offences like cyber terrorism, and redefined intermediary responsibilities.

Does the IT Act apply to offences committed outside India?

Yes, with exceptions — Section 75 gives it extraterritorial reach for offences involving a computer, system, or network located in India.

Who can investigate cybercrime under the Act?

Since the 2008 Amendment, an Inspector-rank officer or higher is authorized to investigate, a change from the earlier DSP-level requirement.
