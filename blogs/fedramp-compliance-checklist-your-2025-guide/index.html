FedRAMP Compliance Checklist: Your 2026 Guide

The 12-step path from impact-level determination to continuous monitoring, and where authorization timelines most often slip.

Deepak Wanage · Updated 2026

Key takeaways

  • FedRAMP authorization is required for cloud providers serving U.S. federal agencies, with an "authorize once, use everywhere" model that lets one authorization serve multiple agencies.
  • The process is resource-intensive — extensive documentation, third-party assessments (3PAOs), and ongoing monitoring.
  • A sponsoring federal agency is mandatory; it reviews the system and issues the Authority to Operate (ATO).
  • Continuous monitoring never stops: monthly vulnerability scans, quarterly reviews, and annual assessments.

Background and impact levels

FedRAMP was established in 2011 by GSA, DHS, and DoD, building on NIST SP 800-53 with cloud-specific additions. Impact levels set the scope of required controls: Low (~125 controls) for public information, Moderate (~325 controls) for Controlled Unclassified Information, and High (400+ controls) for mission-critical or national security data.

The 12-step checklist

  1. Determine impact level: analyze data types, apply FIPS 199 categorization, and document the rationale.
  2. Readiness assessment (optional but recommended): engage a 3PAO to find gaps early and produce a Readiness Assessment Report.
  3. Secure agency sponsorship: partner with an agency, validate categorization, map data flows, and set up communication channels.
  4. Develop the System Security Plan (SSP): document how each control is implemented, with diagrams and supporting appendices.
  5. Implement controls: access management (including PIV/CAC integration), FIPS 140-2 encryption, vulnerability management, and logging.
  6. Third-party assessment: the 3PAO tests controls and produces a Security Assessment Report.
  7. Plan of Action & Milestones (POA&M): track each finding with remediation plans, timelines, and owners.
  8. Submit the authorization package: SSP, SAR, POA&M, and executive attestations for agency and PMO review.
  9. Continuous monitoring: monthly scans, managed changes, annual reassessments, and incident reporting.
  10. Documentation and evidence: automated evidence collection in version-controlled repositories.
  11. Audit trails and logging: capture authentication, privileged access, and configuration events, with protected log integrity and retention.
  12. AI-powered tools (recommended): automation for control tracking, monitoring, reporting, and vulnerability prioritization.

Where authorization timelines slip

  • Underestimating documentation scope, which can delay authorization by six months or more
  • Engaging the sponsoring agency too late in the process
  • Neglecting continuous monitoring and timely remediation, which can lead to suspension or revocation
  • Weak evidence collection systems
  • Poor audit trail and log protection
  • Insufficient in-house cybersecurity expertise
  • Underinvesting in automation

Frequently asked

How long does FedRAMP authorization typically take?

Timelines vary widely by impact level and documentation readiness, but underestimated documentation scope is the single biggest cause of delays, often adding six months or more.

Can we start the process without an agency sponsor?

A readiness assessment can happen first, but formal authorization requires a sponsoring federal agency to review the system and issue the ATO.

Does authorization end once the ATO is granted?

No — continuous monitoring is ongoing, with monthly vulnerability scans, quarterly reviews, and annual reassessments required to maintain authorization.
