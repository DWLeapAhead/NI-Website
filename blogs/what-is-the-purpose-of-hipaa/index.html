What Is the Purpose of HIPAA? A Plain-English Explainer

From its original insurance-portability purpose to the Privacy, Security, and Breach Notification Rules that define compliance today.

Amruta Telang · Updated 2026

What HIPAA is actually for

The Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), passed in 1996, was originally about letting workers keep health insurance coverage when they changed jobs. Its Privacy and Security Rules — the parts most people mean when they say "HIPAA compliance" — came later, establishing national standards for protecting patient health information.

The three rules that matter most

  • The Privacy Rule governs who can access and share Protected Health Information (PHI), and gives patients rights over their own records.
  • The Security Rule requires administrative, physical, and technical safeguards specifically for electronic PHI (ePHI) — access controls, encryption, audit logging, and risk assessments.
  • The Breach Notification Rule sets timelines and requirements for notifying affected individuals, HHS, and sometimes media, when PHI is exposed.

Who HIPAA applies to

Covered entities (health plans, healthcare providers, and healthcare clearinghouses) and their business associates — any vendor that creates, receives, maintains, or transmits PHI on a covered entity's behalf, from billing software to cloud hosting providers.

Why it matters beyond avoiding fines

Beyond civil and criminal penalties for violations, HIPAA compliance is table stakes for doing business in healthcare — most provider and payer contracts require a signed Business Associate Agreement and evidence of a functioning security program before data ever changes hands.

Frequently asked

Does HIPAA apply to all healthcare companies?

It applies to covered entities (health plans, providers, clearinghouses) and their business associates — any vendor handling PHI on their behalf.

What's the difference between the Privacy Rule and the Security Rule?

The Privacy Rule governs who can access and share PHI generally; the Security Rule specifically requires safeguards for electronic PHI, including encryption, access controls, and audit logging.
